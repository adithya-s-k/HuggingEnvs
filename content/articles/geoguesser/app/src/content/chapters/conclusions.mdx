## Takeaways

If you skim one section, make it this one. The sequence to follow is in the introduction; these are the things that actually cost me money, and that I would not have guessed sitting down.

**On the data.** Probe your source before you trust it. An hour spent on 40 OSV-5M sequences told me none of them were spherical, which killed a week of work I had not yet started. And be honest about licences early: Street View has the best coverage in the world and terms that do not allow this, and saying so on day one is much cheaper than discovering it on day thirty.

**On measurement.** Run pass@4, and put a frozen base arm in every sweep. The same untrained model scored between 0.465 and 0.500 across my sweeps, a drift wider than most of the differences I wanted to claim. At pass@1 I briefly believed I had tied Sonnet 5. Then, once you are training, watch `frac_reward_zero_std` rather than reward: if most groups have no spread inside them, no amount of training will help and the loss curve will not tell you. And check what your reward *cannot* distinguish before you tune anything else. Mine flattened to exactly zero past roughly 3,500 km, which covered nearly a third of the untrained model's episodes, so a third of my data carried no gradient at all. Across the nine models I never touched, score also fell with turns per episode at r = −0.75, which was the first hint that deliberation was hurting rather than helping.

**On the runs.** Size `max_completion_length` from a measured p95, because it allocates a logits tensor for the whole budget no matter what the model emits. Alarming training dynamics are a reason to evaluate the checkpoint, not to stop the run: run 1's entropy collapse and grad-norm spike produced two recommendations to kill it, and it was the best run I did. Suppressing exactly those dynamics in run 2 cost four fifths of the gain.

**On yourself.** Distrust a result that arrives without an error. All six of my measurement bugs returned plausible numbers, and exactly one of them ever threw an exception. The 2B adapters served on a 4B base produced a perfectly ordinary looking table of nonsense.

The last one generalises past this project. In RL the feedback loop is long and expensive, so most of the skill is in not fooling yourself between the runs.

## Reproduction

Everything is published, including the raw episode records behind every number above, so nothing here is a screenshot of something you cannot check. The whole set is gathered in **[the GeoGuesser Env collection](https://huggingface.co/collections/HuggingEnvs/geoguesser-env-6a969f8db267fe0e85fa1ab6)**.

**Play it.** [`HuggingEnvs/geoguesser-env`](https://huggingface.co/spaces/HuggingEnvs/geoguesser-env) is the environment as a Space, playable in a browser with the controls exposed. It is the same thing the trainer and the eval talk to.

**The data.** [`HuggingEnvs/geoguesser-tasks`](https://huggingface.co/datasets/HuggingEnvs/geoguesser-tasks) holds both splits: 200 held-out eval tasks across 73 countries, and 3,452 training tasks. It is metadata only, which is what makes it small enough to version. [`HuggingEnvs/geoguesser-panos`](https://huggingface.co/buckets/HuggingEnvs/geoguesser-panos) is the 22 GB of Mapillary imagery, in a Storage Bucket because it is a mutable cache rather than a source; `hf sync` pulls it locally.

**The models.** [`geoguesser-qwen3.5-4b-grpo`](https://huggingface.co/HuggingEnvs/geoguesser-qwen3.5-4b-grpo) is run 1, the adapter behind every number in this article. [`geoguesser-qwen3.5-4b-grpo-v3`](https://huggingface.co/HuggingEnvs/geoguesser-qwen3.5-4b-grpo-v3) is run 3, the ablation that isolated why run 1 worked. Both are LoRA adapters on `Qwen/Qwen3.5-4B`, so they are a few megabytes rather than a few gigabytes.

**The runs.** [`HuggingEnvs/geoguesser-trackio`](https://huggingface.co/spaces/HuggingEnvs/geoguesser-trackio) has all four training runs in one project on a shared axis, and it is embedded below in full, so you can read the curves yourself rather than take my word for the ones I chose to draw.
