## Introduction

There are plenty of RL environments on the internet. Repositories, gyms, benchmark suites, papers with an appendix describing the observation space. What there is very little of is an account of how one got *made*: how you go from a vague idea to a working environment, and then keep iterating on it until a model can be trained to be genuinely good at it.

That gap matters, because a finished environment hides almost everything interesting about building it. Presented as a clean API it looks inevitable. It never was. What the agent may do, what the reward pays for, what a tool is allowed to reveal: every one of those is a judgement call, and most of mine were wrong the first time. Building one takes a lot of intuition and considerably more iteration than the finished thing admits to.

So this is the whole path for one idea, in the order I actually walked it, with the dead ends left in.

The idea: **make a vision-language model play GeoGuessr.** Everything else follows from that. Curating a dataset that makes the task learnable, building the environment, deploying it so other people can use it, running evals worth trusting, and finally training a model that performs well on it. Built with [OpenEnv](https://github.com/huggingface/OpenEnv), trained with [TRL](https://github.com/huggingface/trl).

It worked well enough to be worth writing down. A Qwen3.5-4B with a LoRA adapter finished ahead of `gpt-5.4-mini` and `claude-haiku-4.5` on the held-out split, ahead of every Qwen3.5 up to 397B, and behind only `claude-sonnet-5`. Ten hours on four A100s, around $100, and it runs on a single GPU if you want to try it more cheaply. None of that came from a clever algorithm. It came from the decisions in the sections below, which is why those are the subject rather than the number.

It also ended up somewhere I did not expect. The trained model does not look around more carefully; it looks *less* and answers in one turn, and it is more accurate that way. That is not a random guess and it is not the reward being gamed, and working out which of those it was took me longer than the training did. I go through the evidence later, because that turn is the most useful thing in here.

### What GeoGuessr is

You get dropped somewhere on Earth at street level with no metadata. Turn your head, walk down the road, zoom in on a sign. When you think you know where you are, drop a pin and commit. You are scored on how many kilometres you were off.

Easier to try than to read about. This is the environment itself, which is the same thing a model sees:

Models are bad at this, and bad in a way I found interesting. There is no obvious training set, nobody has optimised against it, and the skill it needs is properly visual and properly sequential. You cannot solve it by thinking harder about one image; you have to go and look at the right thing.

### Why this idea was worth building

Four things make a task worth an environment. They are worth checking against whatever idea you have, because they decide whether the project is tractable before you write any code.

- **The reward is continuous and verifiable.** Distance in kilometres from a known coordinate. No rubric, no LLM judge, nothing to argue with. Every guess gets a gradient rather than a pass or a fail, which matters more than it sounds like it should.
- **It is genuinely multi-turn.** Looking, walking and testing a candidate on the map are separate actions with separate costs. A single-shot version of this is a different and much less interesting problem.
- **It is not saturated.** The best model I measured still misses by a median of 324 km, so there is real headroom to move.
- **It is cheap to simulate.** The whole environment runs on CPU from a local cache, so a rollout can be debugged for pennies before a GPU is involved.

This one happened to be cheap. Most RL environments are not, and are heavily resource dependent.

### How this goes

Here is the order I worked in, and the order I would suggest:

1. **Curate the data.** Where the imagery comes from and what its licence lets you do. I looked at three sources and two of them fall apart the moment you try to train on them.
2. **Build the eval before the training.** A small frozen split and a sweep of models that already exist. That tells you the ceiling, how noisy your measurements are, and which model is worth training at all.
3. **Design the environment.** What tools exist, what they cost, and, the part I nearly got wrong, what they must never reveal.
4. **Deploy it.** Running it in one place is what lets the trainer and the eval talk to exactly the same thing. OpenEnv also makes it shareable: push it to the Hub and anyone can try the environment themselves, in a browser, with nothing installed.
5. **Design the reward, then fix it.** Mine was wrong the first time in a way I only caught by replaying 200 episodes through it.
6. **De-risk before you spend.** Simulate the rollout with no gradient, then overfit four tasks. Both are cheap, and both catch bugs that look identical to a model that cannot learn.
7. **Train, then read the behaviour.** Not just the reward curve. The reward went up for a reason I had not intended, and working out what had happened took longer than the training did.

None of that sequence is specific to GeoGuessr. The order, and the checks at each stage, are what I would apply to any environment; only the details change.

What is specific is how much iteration it took. In this project it came out as three training runs, and they produced very different results from the same environment, the same data and the same base model. Which settings caused that, and why they mattered as much as they did, is the subject of a later section.
